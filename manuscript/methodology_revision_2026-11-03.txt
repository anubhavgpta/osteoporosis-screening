2.2. Outcome Definition
The primary outcome was femoral-neck osteoporosis, defined from DXA-measured bone mineral density (BMD), expressed in g/cm². The study retained the configured equations:
T = (BMD − 0.858) / 0.120
Y = I(T ≤ −2.5)
Here, I denotes the indicator function. The reference mean of 0.858 g/cm² and standard deviation of 0.120 g/cm², also reproduced by Fujiwara et al. [1], were verified directly in Table 3 (p. 476) of “Updated Data on Proximal Femur Bone Mineral Levels of US Adults” by Looker et al. [2]. The table reports these femoral-neck values for 409 non-Hispanic White women aged 20–29 years in NHANES III (1988–1994). Its footnote identifies the estimates as population-weighted. The measurements were obtained using Hologic QDR-1000 DXA densitometers [2].
The same reference values were applied to both sexes to maintain a consistent numerical outcome. ISCD recommends a uniform White female reference for femoral-neck T-scores in men and women [3]. This recommendation supports the reference-population choice; the exact numerical values are supported by Looker et al. [2]. The reference measurements used Hologic QDR-1000 instruments, whereas the 2017–2018 NHANES documentation specifies QDR-4500A densitometers [2,4]. Applying one numerical reference does not establish scanner equivalence across surveys. [AUTHOR VERIFICATION: confirm measurement-scale compatibility across all included NHANES and KNHANES releases.]
This outcome was restricted to the femoral neck and did not classify osteoporosis at the lumbar spine, total hip, or other sites. Osteopenia and normal BMD were negative outcomes in the primary classifier. Women aged 50 years and older were included regardless of documented menopausal status. This age criterion does not establish that all women were postmenopausal. ISCD prefers T-scores in postmenopausal women and men aged at least 50 years, whereas Z-scores are preferred before menopause; WHO criteria may be applied during the menopausal transition [3]. The study label should therefore be interpreted as a research classification rather than a complete clinical diagnosis.
A separate sensitivity analysis used the broader low-BMD outcome, T < −1.0, without replacing the primary endpoint. Because the data were cross-sectional, the models classified current measured status and could not establish future osteoporosis onset or causality.
2.3. Predictor Panels and Preprocessing
The saved E12 classifier used 17 supplied predictors and one derived predictor (Table 1). The supplied variables comprised five demographic, three anthropometric, five history, and four medical or mobility measures. The derived variable combined sex with age groups below 65, 65–74, and 75 years or older. Other experiments evaluated lifestyle, dietary features, dietary-pattern clusters, grip strength, and biomarkers. These experimental predictors were not all included in the saved classifier.
Table 1. Predictor domains in the saved E12 classifier.
Domain | Predictors
Demographic (5) | Age; sex; race/ethnicity; education; poverty-to-income ratio
Anthropometric (3) | BMI; height; weight
History (5) | Prior fracture; parental hip fracture; long-term glucocorticoid use; menopausal status; hormone therapy history
Medical and mobility (4) | Arthritis history; liver condition; thyroid problem; need for walking equipment
Derived (1) | Joint age-group and sex category
Data cleaning distinguished sentinel values indicating refusal or an unknown response from structural skips caused by questionnaire routing. Numerical variables were processed using iterative multivariate imputation with median initialization, a maximum of 15 iterations, and missingness indicators, followed by standardization. Categorical variables used most-frequent imputation and missingness indicators, followed by one-hot encoding. Previously unseen categories were ignored by the encoder rather than assigned a new fitted category.
Preprocessing was included within each model pipeline and learned from the relevant training partition. This does not remove the separate feature-panel selection limitation described in Section 2.5. Dietary clustering, where evaluated, was fitted within training folds. Inspection of the saved classifier confirmed the 18-input contract and these preprocessing settings. For external evaluation, missing KNHANES predictors were handled by the fitted NHANES pipeline; imputation did not make unmeasured predictors equivalent to observed measurements.
2.4. Survey Weighting and Model Development
Non-dietary NHANES analyses used MEC examination weights (WTMEC2YR), consistent with the DXA documentation [4]. Dietary analyses used Day 1 dietary weights (WTDRD1) among participants with a reliable Day 1 recall. For the four production cycles, each two-year weight was divided by four. The preparation code first divided weights by the number of harmonized cycles and then rescaled the retained production cohort to achieve this four-cycle denominator. CDC guidance supports constructing combined weights from the component survey weights [5]. The selected cycles were non-consecutive; the pooled analysis therefore represents these selected periods rather than every year from 2007 to 2018.
Weight scaling was distinct from survey-design handling. The code retained PSU identifiers and added a cycle-specific offset to stratum identifiers. Participant splitting and cross-validation were stratified by outcome, not grouped by PSU. Survey weights were passed to model fitting, scoring, calibration, and performance estimation; this alone does not establish full design-based validity. Numerical scaling was not survey-weighted.
The KNHANES V raw-data user guide, Table 8, specifies BMD pooling factors of 108/580, 200/580, 192/580, and 80/580 for 2008, 2009, 2010, and 2011, respectively [6]. These factors match the harmonization code. The code used wt_ex1 in 2008 and 2011 and wt_itvex in 2009 and 2010. However, Table 4-1 of the guide specifies wt_ex for the 2009 BMD examination; the other three source-weight names agree. The supplied 2009 wt_ex and wt_itvex values differed for all 3,440 eligible participants, and stored pooled weights matched the implemented wt_itvex-based calculation. [AUTHOR VERIFICATION: reconcile the 2009 source weight with the applicable data-release documentation and reassess external estimates before submission. The archived estimates have not been changed.]
The classification routine falls back from a missing requested weight to the MEC weight and then to 1.0. In the available production cohort, all 9,367 MEC weights were finite and positive. All 8,930 participants eligible for dietary analyses had non-missing positive dietary weights, so these fallbacks were not required in those cohorts. The external routine uses unit weights only if the external-weight column is absent; when present, non-finite or non-positive values cause an error. All 9,387 eligible external participants had finite positive supplied weights. These checks concern available files and do not independently recover every historical run.
The main development routine used a stratified 80:20 participant split, five-fold stratified cross-validation, and random seed 42. Candidates were regularized logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost. The saved random forest contained 350 trees and a minimum leaf size of five. Archived metadata and both model-comparison tables identify weighted average precision as the selection metric. In the random-split comparison, random forest had the highest average precision, whereas XGBoost had the highest ROC-AUC, supporting the archived selection record. The current configuration instead specifies ROC-AUC. Probability calibration used a sigmoid method with three internal folds. Calibrated out-of-fold predictions from the training partition determined screening thresholds. The sensitivity-oriented threshold maximized specificity among thresholds reaching at least 90% weighted training sensitivity. Additional thresholds targeted other recall or precision levels. Each selected threshold was applied unchanged to its corresponding evaluation dataset; the training target did not guarantee the same performance in new data.
2.5. Evaluation Designs and Selection Independence
The internal evaluation used 1,874 participants from the stratified participant-level test split. Within the final development routine, model fitting, calibration, and threshold determination used training data only. The temporal evaluation used 7,081 participants from earlier production cycles for development and 2,286 participants from 2017–2018 for evaluation. XGBoost was selected for this temporal experiment. It was a separately trained model, not the identical random forest subsequently evaluated in KNHANES.
The external evaluation applied the frozen NHANES random forest to KNHANES without refitting or recalibration using external outcomes. Previously saved NHANES thresholds were applied unchanged. Separate exploratory experiments involving KNHANES recalibration or combined training were not part of this frozen-model evaluation.
In the main workflow, feature-panel comparisons and selection preceded the final internal or temporal split and used the full classification cohort. Thus, evaluation participants could influence feature-panel selection even though the final fitting routine was training-restricted. The internal and temporal results may consequently contain selection-related optimism and should not be described as fully independent confirmation.
Separate nested leave-one-production-cycle-out experiments withheld one survey cycle at a time. Feature-panel assessment, model choice, hyperparameter tuning, calibration, and threshold selection were conducted within the outer training partitions. These experiments provide a more independent assessment of the tested procedures, but selecting a preferred candidate after reviewing outer-fold results can still introduce optimism. Repeated examination of temporal or external results can also influence later research decisions even when a particular evaluated model remains frozen. The expansion and later recalibration experiments demonstrate such continued exploration; a dated record establishing the full sequence of development decisions and external evaluation remains unavailable.
2.6. Statistical Measures
Performance was assessed using survey-weighted ROC-AUC, average precision, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, calibration, and Brier score. Sensitivity and specificity were the weighted proportions correctly classified among cases and non-cases, respectively. Positive and negative predictive values were the corresponding proportions among positive and negative screens. The repository labels average precision as PR-AUC, but the implemented statistic was average_precision_score: a recall-increment-weighted summary of precision, rather than a trapezoidal integral under the precision–recall curve.
The Brier score was the weighted mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and binary outcomes. Calibration intercept and slope were estimated by survey-weighted logistic regression of the observed outcome on the logit of predicted probability. Probabilities were clipped to 10⁻⁶ and 1 − 10⁻⁶ before this transformation. Calibration assessment describes agreement between probabilities and outcomes and is distinct from the training-stage sigmoid calibration procedure.
Internal 95% confidence intervals used 500 bootstrap iterations. Within each retained survey stratum, the code sampled the observed PSUs with replacement, retaining all evaluated participants and their analysis weights from each selected PSU. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of finite bootstrap estimates defined the interval. Samples without both outcome classes were skipped. The model and thresholds were held fixed; feature selection, model choice, and training were not repeated.
The archived internal and temporal prediction files had complete stratum and PSU identifiers and no strata containing only one observed PSU. Nevertheless, the implementation resampled the retained evaluation subset and did not implement Taylor-series domain variance estimation or a validated survey replicate-weight procedure. CDC guidance distinguishes weighting from variance estimation and emphasizes correct handling of the survey design and analytic subpopulations [7]. These intervals describe uncertainty conditional on the evaluated predictions, not all earlier development decisions. Their design-based coverage remains to be assessed before claims of complete survey-variance adjustment are made.
